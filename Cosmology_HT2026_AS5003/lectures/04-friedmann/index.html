1 · Distances

Distances to a galaxy at redshift $z$

  • Proper distance today (the comoving distance): where the galaxy is now $$d_p(t_0) = r = c\int_{t_e}^{t_0}\frac{dt}{a(t)}$$
  • Proper distance at emission: where the galaxy was when the light left $$d_p(t_e) = a(t_e)\, d_p(t_0) = \frac{d_p(t_0)}{1 + z}$$
  • Light-travel distance: how long the light was on its way (the lookback time $t_0 - t_e$), times $c$ $$d_\text{LT} = c\,(t_0 - t_e)$$
Recap from Tuesday. For a = t/t0 and z = 3 they were 6.1, 1.5 and 3.3 Gpc: three different answers to "how far?". The comoving distance stays the same as the universe expands; with a(t0) = 1 it equals the proper distance today.

The horizon distance

How far away, today, is the farthest point whose light could have reached us since the Big Bang?

$$d_\text{hor}(t_0) = c\int_{0}^{t_0}\frac{dt}{a(t)}$$
  • The proper distance today to the farthest point that could ever have sent us a signal: the size of the observable universe (the particle horizon).
  • It is not measured: it is the limit of what could ever have been in causal contact with us.
  • Whether there is a horizon depends on $a(t)$. For $a \propto t$, $\int_0 dt/t$ diverges: no horizon.
Same integral as the proper distance today, but starting at t = 0. It is a causal limit, not a source we observe: do not read it as "light emitted at the birth of the universe". For our best-fit (benchmark) model it is about 14 Gpc, or 46 billion light years. We need a(t) to evaluate it: that is what the Friedmann equations give us.

Can we see to the horizon?

The cosmic microwave background (CMB)

  • The CMB: a blackbody at $T_0 = 2.7$ K, the same in every direction. It is the oldest light we see.
  • Early on, the universe was a hot, dense plasma. Light kept scattering off free electrons: the universe was opaque, like fog. We cannot see the birth.
  • About 380 000 years after the Big Bang, hydrogen formed and the light escaped. It reaches us now from $z \approx 1100$, just inside the horizon.

Top: COBE/FIRAS spectrum. Bottom: the CMB over the whole sky, from Planck (ESA and the Planck Collaboration, CC BY 4.0)

A quick picture, to answer "can we see the birth of the universe?". The details (recombination, why T ∝ 1/a, the spots) come in the CMB lecture (Ryden ch. 8). Opaque like fog, or the inside of the Sun: light cannot travel far before it scatters off a free electron. Once neutral hydrogen forms, the photons hardly interact again. In the benchmark model the last-scattering surface is now about 13.9 Gpc away and the horizon about 14.2 Gpc: in light we see about 98% of the way to the horizon, but not beyond the CMB. (Neutrinos or gravitational waves could, in principle, come from earlier.)

2 · Einstein's field equation

Newton vs. Einstein

The expansion of the universe is regulated by gravity.

Newtonian gravity

$$\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho, \qquad \frac{d^2\vec r}{dt^2} = -\vec\nabla\Phi$$

Mass density $\rho$ sets the potential $\Phi$; the potential sets how things accelerate.

General relativity

$$G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}$$

Energy and pressure ($T_{\mu\nu}$) curve spacetime ($G_{\mu\nu}$); the curvature sets how things move.

In Newtonian gravity only mass density is a source. In general relativity energy density and pressure both gravitate; this will matter for the acceleration equation.

Einstein's field equation

$$G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}$$

Left: geometry of spacetime.
"Spacetime tells matter how to move."

Right: contents of the universe (energy density, pressure, …).
"Matter tells spacetime how to curve."

$$R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 R\, g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}$$

$g_{\mu\nu}$: the metric. The Ricci tensor $R_{\mu\nu}$ and Ricci scalar $R$ depend only on the metric and its first and second derivatives.

Wheeler's line from Tuesday, now as an equation. G_μν is the Einstein tensor; T_μν the stress–energy tensor.

Einstein's static universe

In 1917 everyone assumed a static universe. But with matter alone there is no static solution: gravity only attracts, so a universe at rest collapses. Einstein added a repulsive term to the geometry side, the cosmological constant $\Lambda$:

$$R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 R\, g_{\mu\nu} + \textcolor{#ea580c}{\Lambda\, g_{\mu\nu}} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}$$
UniverseNeed $\Lambda$?
Static (1917)Yes, exactly balancing gravity
Expanding, slowing down (Hubble, 1929)No: Einstein dropped it, reportedly his "biggest blunder"
Expanding, speeding up (supernovae, 1998)Yes, and it is back

Today $\Lambda$ sits on the other side, as something the universe contains:

$$R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 R\, g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu} \textcolor{#ea580c}{\,-\,\Lambda\, g_{\mu\nu}}$$
Einstein (1917). Nobody knew yet that the universe expands; it was not even settled that other galaxies exist (the "Great Debate", 1920). Row 2: expansion needs no push. Like a ball thrown upwards: gravity pulls it back all the time, yet it keeps rising, more and more slowly, thanks to the initial throw. A matter-only universe can expand the same way (Friedmann 1922 found such solutions). Einstein dropped Λ around 1931. Eddington (1930) had also shown the static universe is unstable: a little more matter and it collapses, a little less and it runs away. The "biggest blunder" quote reaches us second hand, through Gamow. Row 3: repulsion is needed for acceleration, not for expansion. In 1998 supernovae showed that the expansion speeds up (Nobel Prize 2011): Λ came back, larger than Einstein's static value. Last click: moved across the equals sign, Λ is no longer geometry but a content of the universe, a vacuum energy with P = −ε. We come back to it at the end of today.

In a homogeneous and isotropic universe

$$R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 R\, g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}$$

In general: ten coupled, second-order, nonlinear differential equations.

For a homogeneous and isotropic universe they simplify a lot:

  • The metric is the Robertson–Walker metric: only $a(t)$, $\kappa$ and $R_0$
  • The stress–energy tensor can only depend on time

3 · The Friedmann equation

The Friedmann equation

From the time–time (00) component of Einstein's equation:

$$\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\textcolor{#dc2626}{\varepsilon(t)} - \frac{\textcolor{#2563eb}{\kappa}\, c^2}{\textcolor{#2563eb}{R_0^2}}\,\frac{1}{a(t)^2}$$
  • $\varepsilon(t)$: the energy density of everything in the universe
  • $\kappa$, $R_0$: the curvature of space

Or, with the Hubble parameter $H(t) = \dot a/a$:

$$H(t)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon(t) - \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}\,\frac{1}{a(t)^2}$$
Friedmann derived it in 1922 (positive curvature) and 1924 (negative curvature); Lemaître found it independently in 1927. The expansion rate is set by what is in the universe and by its curvature.

The critical density

For a flat universe ($\kappa = 0$):

$$H(t)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon(t) \underbrace{\cancel{\;-\,\frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}\,\frac{1}{a(t)^2}\;}}_{=\,0}$$

The critical density, for which the universe is flat:

$$\varepsilon_c(t) \equiv \frac{3c^2 H(t)^2}{8\pi G}$$

Today, with $H_0 = 68$ km/s/Mpc:

$$\varepsilon_c(t_0) = \frac{3c^2 H_0^2}{8\pi G} \approx 4900\ \text{MeV m}^{-3}, \qquad \rho_c = \frac{\varepsilon_c}{c^2} \approx 9\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$$

About 5 protons per cubic metre.

H0 = 68 km/s/Mpc = 2.20×10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. ρ_c = 3H0²/(8πG) = 8.7×10⁻²⁷ kg/m³; ε_c = 7.8×10⁻¹⁰ J/m³ = 4870 MeV/m³. A proton is 938 MeV: about 5 per cubic metre.

The density parameter $\Omega$

$$\Omega(t) \equiv \frac{\varepsilon(t)}{\varepsilon_c(t)}$$

Start from the Friedmann equation:

$$H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon - \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2\, a^2}$$

Divide by $H^2$, using $\dfrac{8\pi G}{3c^2 H^2} = \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_c}$:

$$1 = \underbrace{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_c}}_{\Omega} - \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2\, a^2 H^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 1 - \Omega(t) = -\frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2\, a(t)^2\, H(t)^2}$$

The right-hand side cannot change sign with time, so neither can the left (cases on the right).

  • $\Omega < 1$: $\kappa = -1$, negatively curved
  • $\Omega > 1$: $\kappa = +1$, positively curved
  • $\Omega = 1$: $\kappa = 0$, flat

NASA / WMAP Science Team

The density of the universe decides its geometry. The figure is the cover of Lecture 3: the Ω labels make sense now. Measurements (CMB) give Ω very close to 1 today: the universe is flat, or very nearly so.

4 · Fluid and acceleration equations

The fluid equation

The Friedmann equation is one equation with two unknowns, $a(t)$ and $\varepsilon(t)$. We need another: the fluid equation.

First law of thermodynamics for a comoving box (no heat flows in a homogeneous universe, $dQ = 0$): $$dE + P\,dV = dQ = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot E + P\,\dot V = 0$$

With $V \propto a^3$, so $\dot V/V = 3\,\dot a/a$, and $E = V\varepsilon$: $$\dot E = V\left(\dot\varepsilon + 3\frac{\dot a}{a}\,\varepsilon\right)$$

$$\dot\varepsilon + 3\,\frac{\dot a}{a}\,(\varepsilon + P) = 0$$

The box expands with the universe. Its energy changes only through the work done by the pressure, P dV. Substituting: V(ε̇ + 3(ȧ/a)ε) + P·3(ȧ/a)V = 0, divide by V.

Activity in pairs

Derive the acceleration equation

In pairs, combine the Friedmann equation and the fluid equation, $$\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon - \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}\,\frac{1}{a^2}, \qquad \dot\varepsilon + 3\,\frac{\dot a}{a}\,(\varepsilon + P) = 0,$$ to show that $$\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3c^2}\,(\varepsilon + 3P)$$

Hints: multiply the Friedmann equation by $a^2$; take the time derivative (the curvature term is a constant); use the fluid equation to replace $\dot\varepsilon$.

Work in pairs. Show the hints if pairs are stuck. Then derive on the board: 2ȧä = (8πG/3c²)(ε̇a² + 2εaȧ). Divide by 2aȧ: ä/a = (4πG/3c²)(ε̇ a/ȧ + 2ε). Fluid equation: ε̇ a/ȧ = −3(ε + P). So ä/a = (4πG/3c²)(−3ε − 3P + 2ε) = −(4πG/3c²)(ε + 3P). It also follows directly from the space–space components of Einstein's equation: "Friedmann's second equation". Pressure makes the deceleration larger: in GR, pressure gravitates.

5 · Equations of state

Equations of state

$$\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon - \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}\,\frac{1}{a^2}, \qquad \dot\varepsilon + 3\,\frac{\dot a}{a}\,(\varepsilon + P) = 0$$

Two equations, three unknowns: $a$, $\varepsilon$ and $P$. We need an equation of state $P(\varepsilon)$ for each component.

For the components of the universe relevant to cosmology it takes a simple form:

$$P = w\,\varepsilon$$

with $w$ a dimensionless number.

Nonrelativistic matter: $w \approx 0$

Ideal gas: $\;P = \dfrac{\rho}{\mu}\,kT$

With $\varepsilon = \rho c^2$ and $\dfrac{kT}{\mu} = \dfrac{\langle v^2\rangle}{3}$: $$P = \frac{\langle v^2\rangle}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon, \qquad w = \frac{\langle v^2\rangle}{3c^2}$$

For nonrelativistic particles $\langle v^2\rangle \ll c^2$, so $w = 0$ is a good approximation. (For the air in this room, $w \approx 10^{-12}$.)

μ is the mean mass per particle. Air molecules move at about 500 m/s: ⟨v²⟩/3c² ≈ 10⁻¹². Matter here means anything moving slowly: atoms, and dark matter too.

Relativistic gas (photons, neutrinos): $w = 1/3$

Photons with energy $hc/\lambda$ and number density $n$ (for a blackbody):

$$\varepsilon = n\left\langle\frac{hc}{\lambda}\right\rangle = \alpha T^4, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\pi^2 k^4}{15\,\hbar^3 c^3}$$
$$P = \frac13\, n\left\langle\frac{hc}{\lambda}\right\rangle = \frac13\,\alpha T^4$$ $$w = \frac{P}{\varepsilon} = \frac13$$
The factor 1/3: the momentum flux of particles moving at c in random directions, one third per direction. Neutrinos count as radiation as long as they are relativistic.

Dark energy: $w < -1/3$

Acceleration equation with $P = w\varepsilon$:

$$\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3c^2}\,(\varepsilon + 3P) = -\frac{4\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon\,(1 + 3w)$$

If $w < -\tfrac13$, the right-hand side is positive: $\ddot a > 0$, an accelerating universe.

Any component with $w < -\tfrac13$ is called dark energy. The best-known case, $w = -1$, is a cosmological constant.

Literature topic: evidence for evolving dark energy (e.g. DESI).

Matter (w = 0) and radiation (w = 1/3) both decelerate the expansion. Something with negative pressure is needed to accelerate it.

The cosmological constant, revisited

With Einstein's $\Lambda$ in the field equation, the acceleration equation becomes

$$\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3c^2}\,(\varepsilon + 3P) + \frac{\Lambda}{3}$$

For nonrelativistic matter ($P = 0$, $\varepsilon = \rho c^2$):

$$\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\,\rho + \frac{\Lambda}{3}$$

so a static universe ($\ddot a = 0$) needs $\Lambda = 4\pi G\rho$.

Einstein's static universe. It is unstable: a little more matter and it collapses, a little less and it expands.

The cosmological constant as an energy density

The Friedmann equation with $\Lambda$:

$$\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon - \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}\,\frac{1}{a^2} + \frac{\Lambda}{3}$$

$\Lambda$ acts like a component with a constant energy density: $$\varepsilon_\Lambda = \frac{c^2}{8\pi G}\,\Lambda$$

Constant, so $\dot\varepsilon_\Lambda = 0$ and the fluid equation gives $\varepsilon_\Lambda + P_\Lambda = 0$: $$P_\Lambda = -\varepsilon_\Lambda, \qquad w_\Lambda = -1$$

Summary: the equations for $a(t)$

Friedmann: $\;\left(\dfrac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon - \dfrac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}\,\dfrac{1}{a^2}$

Fluid: $\;\dot\varepsilon + 3\,\dfrac{\dot a}{a}\,(\varepsilon + P) = 0$

Acceleration: $\;\dfrac{\ddot a}{a} = -\dfrac{4\pi G}{3c^2}\,(\varepsilon + 3P)$

Equation of state: $\;P = w\,\varepsilon$
