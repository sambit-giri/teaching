The Friedmann equation with $\Omega$

The curvature term, in terms of $\Omega_0$

The Friedmann equation: $\quad\displaystyle H(t)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon(t) - \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2\, a(t)^2}$

With the density parameter $\Omega \equiv \varepsilon/\varepsilon_c$ and $\varepsilon_c = \dfrac{3c^2H^2}{8\pi G}$ (discussed in Lecture 4):

$$1 - \Omega(t) = -\frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2\, a(t)^2\, H(t)^2}$$
$$\xrightarrow{\;a\,=\,1,\;\; H\,=\,H_0,\;\; \Omega\,=\,\Omega_0\;}\quad \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2} = H_0^2\,(\Omega_0 - 1)$$

Put this into the Friedmann equation and divide by $H_0^2$, using $\varepsilon_{c,0} = \dfrac{3c^2H_0^2}{8\pi G}$:

$$\frac{H(t)^2}{H_0^2} = \frac{\varepsilon(t)}{\varepsilon_{c,0}} + \frac{1 - \Omega_0}{a(t)^2}$$
Ω0 is the total density parameter today, of all components together. Curvature is now written with quantities we can measure: H0 and Ω0.

The Friedmann equation with all components

$\varepsilon$ is the total energy density:

$$\varepsilon(t) = \frac{\varepsilon_{r,0}}{a^4} + \frac{\varepsilon_{m,0}}{a^3} + \varepsilon_{\Lambda,0}$$

Divide by $\varepsilon_{c,0}$, with $\Omega_{i,0} = \varepsilon_{i,0}/\varepsilon_{c,0}$:

$$\frac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \htmlClass{c-hot}{\underbrace{\frac{\Omega_{r,0}}{a^4}}_{\text{radiation}}} + \htmlClass{c-accent}{\underbrace{\frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3}}_{\text{matter}}} + \htmlClass{c-good}{\underbrace{\Omega_{\Lambda,0}}_{\Lambda}} + \htmlClass{c-soft}{\underbrace{\frac{1 - \Omega_0}{a^2}}_{\text{curvature}}}$$

with $\Omega_0 = \Omega_{r,0} + \Omega_{m,0} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}$.

Each term goes as a different power of $a$, so each has its own era in which it dominates.

The general solution

With $H = \dot a/a$, so $dt = da/(aH)$, the Friedmann equation gives

$$H_0\, dt = \frac{da}{\left[\Omega_{r,0}\,a^{-2} + \Omega_{m,0}\,a^{-1} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\,a^2 + (1 - \Omega_0)\right]^{1/2}}$$

Integrate from the Big Bang ($a = 0$):

$$H_0\, t = \int_0^a \frac{da'}{\left[\Omega_{r,0}\,a'^{-2} + \Omega_{m,0}\,a'^{-1} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\,a'^2 + (1 - \Omega_0)\right]^{1/2}}$$

In general this must be integrated numerically. The same holds for distances, e.g. the comoving distance (exercise sheet on multiple-component universes): $$d_p(t_0) = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_{a_e}^1 \frac{da}{\left[\Omega_{r,0} + \Omega_{m,0}\,a + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\,a^4 + (1 - \Omega_0)\,a^2\right]^{1/2}}$$

From H²/H0² = E(a)²: dt = da/(a H0 E), and a E = [Ωr a⁻² + Ωm a⁻¹ + ΩΛ a² + (1 − Ω0)]^(1/2). For the distance, d_p(t0) = c∫dt/a = c∫da/(a² H) = (c/H0)∫da/(a² E).

The most popular model until 1998

Matter + curvature

The universe contains only matter, so $\Omega_0 = \Omega_{m,0}$:

$$\frac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3} + \frac{1 - \Omega_{m,0}}{a^2}$$
  • $\Omega_{m,0} = 1$: flat. The Einstein–de Sitter universe, $a \propto t^{2/3}$.
  • $\Omega_{m,0} < 1$ ($\kappa = -1$): the right side is always positive, so the universe expands forever. The matter thins out until the curvature term dominates: $a \propto t$, like the empty universe.
  • $\Omega_{m,0} > 1$ ($\kappa = +1$): the curvature term is negative.
    $H = 0$ (or $\dot a = 0$) at $a_\text{max} = \dfrac{\Omega_{m,0}}{\Omega_{m,0} - 1}$, and then the universe contracts, the mirror image of its expansion: a Big Crunch.

Density is destiny: $\Omega_{m,0}$ alone sets both the geometry and the fate.

Future:

The plot starts at Ωm,0 = 1 (Einstein–de Sitter, on the blue line); move the slider for each case as you talk about it. Grey: the empty universe, Ωm,0 = 0, a straight line. a_max: set Ωm,0/a³ = (Ωm,0 − 1)/a². After a_max the universe retraces its expansion in reverse, ending at a = 0 a time 2 t_max after the Big Bang. Check a_max on the slider: Ωm,0 = 2 gives a_max = 2. Until 1998 this was the whole question: is Ω0 above or below 1? The amount of matter decided both the geometry and the fate. In 1998–99 two supernova teams (Riess et al. 1998, Perlmutter et al. 1999) found that the expansion is speeding up, which needs Λ (Nobel Prize in Physics 2011: Perlmutter, Schmidt, Riess). With Λ, the link between geometry and fate breaks (next slides).

Close to our universe today

Matter + $\Lambda$, flat

Flat, so $\Omega_{m,0} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0} = 1$:

$$\frac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3} + (1 - \Omega_{m,0})$$
  • $\Omega_{m,0} < 1$: the right side is always positive. The universe expands forever, faster and faster: the Big Chill.
  • $\Omega_{m,0} > 1$ needs $\Omega_{\Lambda,0} < 0$: a $\Lambda$ that attracts. There is a maximum $a$, and then a Big Crunch. What is $a_\text{max}$?

Future:

Starts at Ωm,0 = 0.31, ΩΛ,0 = 0.69: the Benchmark Model. Grey and blue: the empty and Einstein–de Sitter universes, as on the previous slide. a_max: H = 0 where Ωm,0/a³ = Ωm,0 − 1. Let the students work it out: a_max = (Ωm,0/(Ωm,0 − 1))^(1/3); check it on the slider (Ωm,0 = 1.2 gives a_max = 1.82). Ryden's examples: Ωm,0 = 0.9 (Big Chill, close to Einstein–de Sitter for a long time) and Ωm,0 = 1.1, ΩΛ,0 = −0.1 (Big Crunch). At late times Λ dominates and the universe approaches de Sitter: exponential expansion.

Matter + $\Lambda$ + curvature

$$\frac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0} + \frac{1 - \Omega_{m,0} - \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}{a^2}$$

Many behaviours are possible, depending on $\Omega_{m,0}$ and $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$:

  • Big Chill: expands forever
  • Big Crunch: expands, then recollapses
  • Loitering: $a$ stays almost constant for a long time, then expands again
  • Big Bounce: no Big Bang. The universe contracts, bounces, and expands
All four have Ωm,0 = 0.31 (Ryden Fig. 5.7). First click: Big Chill and Big Crunch, met already. Second click: "There are two more interesting cases." Loitering: matter and Λ almost balance, as in Einstein's static universe; Lemaître discussed it in 1927. A Big Bounce universe has a minimum scale factor, so a maximum redshift: in Ryden's example z_max ≈ 0.8. We see quasars at z > 6, so we do not live in one.

A good approximation for the early universe

Radiation + matter, flat

$$\frac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \frac{\Omega_{r,0}}{a^4} + \frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3}$$

Our universe: $\Omega_{r,0} = 9.0\times10^{-5}$, $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.31$ ($\Lambda$ and curvature are negligible at $a \ll 1$).

At early times ($a \ll a_{rm}$) radiation dominates, $a \propto t^{1/2}$; later matter takes over, $a \propto t^{2/3}$.

It can be integrated exactly (Ryden eq. 5.110):

$$H_0\, t = \frac{4a_{rm}^2}{3\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}}}\left[1 - \left(1 - \frac{a}{2a_{rm}}\right)\left(1 + \frac{a}{a_{rm}}\right)^{1/2}\right]$$

At $a = a_{rm} = \Omega_{r,0}/\Omega_{m,0} = 2.9\times10^{-4}$: the time of radiation–matter equality, $\;t_{rm} \approx 3.5\times10^{-6}\,H_0^{-1} \approx 50\,000$ years. A short phase, cosmologically speaking, but a very interesting one.

The plot has log axes: a power law is a straight line, with slope 1/2 for radiation and 2/3 for matter. The exact solution (white) bends from one to the other around equality (gold), with Ωr,0 = 9.0×10⁻⁵ and Ωm,0 = 0.31. The radiation era is so short that leaving out radiation changes the age of the universe by only a few parts per million. "Very interesting": the first nuclei (Lecture 12) form during it, and the CMB (Lectures 10–11) comes from shortly after.
