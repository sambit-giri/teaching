1 · The Friedmann equation with $\Omega$

The curvature term, in terms of $\Omega_0$

From Lecture 4:

$$1 - \Omega(t) = -\frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2\, a(t)^2\, H(t)^2}$$

Today ($a = 1$, $H = H_0$, $\Omega = \Omega_0$):

$$\frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2} = H_0^2\,(\Omega_0 - 1)$$

Put this into the Friedmann equation and divide by $H_0^2$, using $\varepsilon_{c,0} = \dfrac{3c^2H_0^2}{8\pi G}$:

$$\frac{H(t)^2}{H_0^2} = \frac{\varepsilon(t)}{\varepsilon_{c,0}} + \frac{1 - \Omega_0}{a(t)^2}$$
Ω0 is the total density parameter today, of all components together. Curvature is now written with quantities we can measure: H0 and Ω0.

The Friedmann equation with all components

$\varepsilon$ is the total energy density:

$$\varepsilon(t) = \frac{\varepsilon_{r,0}}{a^4} + \frac{\varepsilon_{m,0}}{a^3} + \varepsilon_{\Lambda,0}$$

Divide by $\varepsilon_{c,0}$, with $\Omega_{i,0} = \varepsilon_{i,0}/\varepsilon_{c,0}$:

$$\frac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \underbrace{\frac{\Omega_{r,0}}{a^4}}_{\text{radiation}} + \underbrace{\frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3}}_{\text{matter}} + \underbrace{\Omega_{\Lambda,0}}_{\Lambda} + \underbrace{\frac{1 - \Omega_0}{a^2}}_{\text{curvature}}$$

with $\Omega_0 = \Omega_{r,0} + \Omega_{m,0} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}$.

Each term goes as a different power of $a$, so each has its own era in which it dominates.

2 · The general solution

The general solution

With $H = \dot a/a$, so $dt = da/(aH)$, the Friedmann equation gives

$$H_0\, dt = \frac{da}{\left[\Omega_{r,0}\,a^{-2} + \Omega_{m,0}\,a^{-1} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\,a^2 + (1 - \Omega_0)\right]^{1/2}}$$

Integrate from the Big Bang ($a = 0$):

$$H_0\, t = \int_0^a \frac{da'}{\left[\Omega_{r,0}\,a'^{-2} + \Omega_{m,0}\,a'^{-1} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\,a'^2 + (1 - \Omega_0)\right]^{1/2}}$$

In general this must be integrated numerically. The same holds for distances, e.g. the comoving distance (exercises): $$d_p(t_0) = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_{a_e}^1 \frac{da}{\left[\Omega_{r,0} + \Omega_{m,0}\,a + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\,a^4 + (1 - \Omega_0)\,a^2\right]^{1/2}}$$

From H²/H0² = E(a)²: dt = da/(a H0 E), and a E = [Ωr a⁻² + Ωm a⁻¹ + ΩΛ a² + (1 − Ω0)]^(1/2). For the distance, d_p(t0) = c∫dt/a = c∫da/(a² H) = (c/H0)∫da/(a² E).

3 · Matter + curvature: the model before 1998

Matter + curvature

$$\frac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \frac{\Omega_0}{a^3} + \frac{1 - \Omega_0}{a^2}$$
  • $\Omega_0 = 1$: flat. The Einstein–de Sitter universe, $a \propto t^{2/3}$.
  • $\Omega_0 < 1$ ($\kappa = -1$): the right side is always positive, so the universe expands forever. The matter thins out until the curvature term dominates: $a \propto t$, like the empty universe.
  • $\Omega_0 > 1$ ($\kappa = +1$): the curvature term is negative. $H = 0$ at $$a_\text{max} = \frac{\Omega_0}{\Omega_0 - 1}$$ and then the universe contracts, the mirror image of its expansion: a Big Crunch.
a_max: set Ω0/a³ = (Ω0 − 1)/a². After a_max the universe retraces its expansion in reverse, ending at a = 0 a time 2 t_max after the Big Bang.

Matter + curvature

Try it: density is destiny

Dashed: the Einstein–de Sitter universe, $\Omega_0 = 1$.

Until 1998 this was the whole question: is Ω0 above or below 1? The amount of matter decided both the geometry and the fate. With Λ, that link breaks (next slides). Slide to Ω0 = 0: the empty universe, a straight line.

Matter + Λ, flat: close to our universe today

Matter + $\Lambda$, flat

Flat, so $\Omega_{m,0} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0} = 1$:

$$\frac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3} + (1 - \Omega_{m,0})$$
  • $\Omega_{m,0} < 1$: the right side is always positive. The universe expands forever, faster and faster: the Big Chill.
  • $\Omega_{m,0} > 1$ needs $\Omega_{\Lambda,0} < 0$: a $\Lambda$ that attracts. There is a maximum $a$, and then a Big Crunch.
Ryden's examples: Ωm,0 = 0.9 (Big Chill, close to Einstein–de Sitter for a long time) and Ωm,0 = 1.1, ΩΛ,0 = −0.1 (Big Crunch). At late times Λ dominates and the universe approaches de Sitter: exponential expansion.

Matter + $\Lambda$ + curvature

$$\frac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0} + \frac{1 - \Omega_{m,0} - \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}{a^2}$$

Many behaviours are possible, depending on $\Omega_{m,0}$ and $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$:

  • Big Chill: expands forever
  • Big Crunch: expands, then recollapses
  • Loitering: $a$ stays almost constant for a long time, then expands again
  • Big Bounce: no Big Bang. The universe contracts, bounces, and expands
Loitering: matter and Λ almost balance, as in Einstein's static universe; Lemaître discussed it in 1927. A Big Bounce universe has a minimum scale factor, so a maximum redshift: in Ryden's example z_max ≈ 0.8. We see quasars at z > 6, so we do not live in one.

Radiation + matter, flat: the early universe

Radiation + matter

$$\frac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \frac{\Omega_{r,0}}{a^4} + \frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3}$$

At early times ($a \ll a_{rm}$) radiation dominates, $a \propto t^{1/2}$; later matter takes over, $a \propto t^{2/3}$.

It can be integrated exactly (Ryden eq. 5.110):

$$H_0\, t = \frac{4a_{rm}^2}{3\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}}}\left[1 - \left(1 - \frac{a}{2a_{rm}}\right)\left(1 + \frac{a}{a_{rm}}\right)^{1/2}\right]$$

At $a = a_{rm} = 2.9\times10^{-4}$: the time of radiation–matter equality, $\;t_{rm} \approx 3.5\times10^{-6}\,H_0^{-1} \approx 50\,000$ years. A short phase, cosmologically speaking, but a very interesting one.

The radiation era is so short that leaving out radiation changes the age of the universe by only a few parts per million. "Very interesting": the first nuclei (Lecture 12) form during it, and the CMB (Lectures 10–11) comes from shortly after.

On logarithmic axes the Benchmark Model looks like a sequence of single-component universes: $a \propto t^{1/2}$, then $t^{2/3}$, then exponential.

Linear axes first: today, and the switch from slowing down to speeding up near matter–Λ equality (the curve bends upwards). Then log axes: straight lines of slope 1/2 and 2/3, and the turn upwards at the end. Each era is well described by last lecture's solutions.

Distances in the Benchmark Model

Benchmark Model, flat matter only and flat $\Lambda$ only, all with the same $H_0$. At small $z$ they agree: Hubble's law, $d_p \approx cz/H_0$.

At emission, distance has a maximum: the most distant galaxies were close to us when their light left.

After Ryden Fig. 5.9. Today: the Benchmark curve flattens towards its horizon, about 3.2 c/H0 = 14 Gpc. At emission: in the Benchmark Model the maximum is near z ≈ 1.6, at about 0.4 c/H0 ≈ 1.8 Gpc. The models differ most at z ~ 1: measuring distances out there tells them apart (Lecture 7).

Summary: universes with several components

  • $\dfrac{H^2}{H_0^2} = \dfrac{\Omega_{r,0}}{a^4} + \dfrac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0} + \dfrac{1 - \Omega_0}{a^2}$, solved for $a(t)$ numerically in general
  • Matter + curvature: density is destiny. With $\Lambda$, the fate depends on both $\Omega_{m,0}$ and $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$
  • The Benchmark Model (ΛCDM): flat, $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.31$, $\Omega_{\Lambda,0} = 0.69$; radiation, then matter, then $\Lambda$ dominate; $t_0 = 13.7$ Gyr
