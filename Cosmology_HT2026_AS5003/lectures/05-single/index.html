1 · Energy density and scale factor

How does $\varepsilon$ change with $a$?

Put the equation of state $P = w\varepsilon$ into the fluid equation:

$$\dot\varepsilon + 3\,\frac{\dot a}{a}\,(1 + w)\,\varepsilon = 0$$

Separate the variables and integrate:

$$\frac{d\varepsilon}{\varepsilon} = -3(1 + w)\,\frac{da}{a}$$ $$\Rightarrow\quad \textcolor{#ea580c}{\varepsilon(a) = \varepsilon_0\, a^{-3(1+w)}}$$

Matter$0$$\varepsilon_{m,0}\, a^{-3}$
Radiation$1/3$$\varepsilon_{r,0}\, a^{-4}$
Cosmological
constant		$-1$$\varepsilon_{\Lambda,0}$:

ε0 is the value today, since a(t0) = 1. This holds for each component separately, as long as the components do not exchange energy.

Building intuition for these powers of $a$

  • Matter: the number of particles per volume falls as $a^{-3}$; each keeps its energy $mc^2$. So $\varepsilon_m \propto a^{-3}$.
  • Radiation: the number of photons per volume also falls as $a^{-3}$, but each photon is stretched: $E = hc/\lambda \propto a^{-1}$. So $\varepsilon_r \propto a^{-4}$.
  • Cosmological constant: the energy of empty space itself. More space, same energy per volume: $\varepsilon_\Lambda$ = constant.
The photon picture is the redshift from Lecture 2: λ grows with a, so each photon loses energy. Matter particles are not stretched (their kinetic energy does fall, but for nonrelativistic matter it is negligible next to mc²). For Λ: the fluid equation with P = −ε gives ε̇ = 0 directly. Energy is not conserved in the usual sense in an expanding universe: the negative pressure does work.

2 · Which component dominates?

The mix changes over time

$$\varepsilon(a) = \varepsilon_0\, a^{-3(1+w)}$$

The larger $w$, the faster $\varepsilon$ falls as the universe expands.

  • As $a \to 0$: the component with the highest $w$ dominates.
  • As $a \to \infty$: the component with the lowest $w$ dominates.

Our universe has gone from radiation-dominated, to matter-dominated, to dark energy-dominated.

Plotted for the benchmark model (Ryden): Ωr,0 = 9×10⁻⁵, Ωm,0 = 0.31, ΩΛ,0 = 0.69, in units of today's critical density. On a log–log plot each component is a straight line with slope −3(1 + w). Where two lines cross, the two components have the same energy density: the next two slides.

Radiation–matter equality

$$\varepsilon_m(a) = \varepsilon_{m,0}\, a^{-3}, \qquad \varepsilon_r(a) = \varepsilon_{r,0}\, a^{-4}$$

Today $\;\Omega_{m,0} \equiv \dfrac{\varepsilon_{m,0}}{\varepsilon_{c,0}} = 0.31\;$ and $\;\Omega_{r,0} \equiv \dfrac{\varepsilon_{r,0}}{\varepsilon_{c,0}} = 9\times10^{-5}$.

At what scale factor $a$ was $\varepsilon_m(a) = \varepsilon_r(a)$?

Divide both sides by $\varepsilon_{c,0}$:

$$\Omega_{m,0}\, a^{-3} = \Omega_{r,0}\, a^{-4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad a_{rm} = \frac{\Omega_{r,0}}{\Omega_{m,0}} = \frac{9\times10^{-5}}{0.31} \approx 2.9\times10^{-4}$$

That is redshift $z_{rm} = 1/a_{rm} - 1 \approx 3400$.

Try in pairs

Matter–Λ equality

$$\varepsilon_m(a) = \varepsilon_{m,0}\, a^{-3}, \qquad \varepsilon_\Lambda(a) = \varepsilon_{\Lambda,0}$$

Today $\;\Omega_{m,0} = 0.31\;$ and $\;\Omega_{\Lambda,0} = 0.69$.

  • At what scale factor $a$ was $\varepsilon_m(a) = \varepsilon_\Lambda(a)$?
  • What redshift $z$ does this correspond to?
Answer: a = (Ωm,0/ΩΛ,0)^(1/3) = 0.77, z = 0.30 (Ryden Table 5.2). Cosmologically very recent: dark energy has dominated only for the last few billion years. Extra, if time: the expansion started to accelerate a bit earlier, when εm = 2εΛ (ä = 0 needs ε + 3P = 0): a = (Ωm,0/2ΩΛ,0)^(1/3) = 0.61, z = 0.64.

3 · The empty universe

The empty (Milne) universe

The Friedmann equation, multiplied by $a^2$:

$$\dot a^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon\, a^2 - \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}$$
$$\dot a^2 = \cancel{\frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon\, a^2} - \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}$$
$$\xrightarrow{\;\varepsilon\,=\,0\;}\quad \textcolor{#ea580c}{\dot a^2 = -\frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}}$$
  • $\kappa = 0$: $\dot a = 0$, a static, empty, flat universe. Allowed, but not very interesting.
  • $\kappa = -1$: $\dot a = c/R_0$, so $\;a(t) = \dfrac{t}{t_0}\;$ with $\;t_0 = \dfrac{R_0}{c}$, and $H_0 = 1/t_0$.

This is the $a = t/t_0$ we used for distances in Lecture 3. It has no horizon (Lecture 4).

What about $\kappa = +1$?

Before the click: the full Friedmann equation with all components together has no simple solution. But if one component dominates, we can drop the others, and then the equation can be solved exactly. So we build model universes one component at a time, starting with the simplest: nothing at all, ε = 0. κ = +1: ask the class before showing the answer (ȧ² would be negative). Nothing slows the expansion down, so the universe coasts: ȧ is constant and the age is exactly the Hubble time. Named after Edward Milne (1932). The galaxies are only test particles in it.

The empty universe in pictures

Left: a(t) with time in Hubble times, today at 0; the Big Bang is exactly 1/H0 ago. Middle: proper distance of a galaxy at redshift z, today (Observation) and when the light left it (Emission), in units of c/H0, on log axes as in Ryden Fig. 5.3. Right: the spacetime diagram of this universe (as in Lectures 3 and 4), with a galaxy at z = 2: blue is its distance today, orange its distance at emission, gold our past light cone. Today's distance grows without limit as z grows: no horizon. The distance at emission has a maximum: the most distant galaxies were close to us when their light left.

4 · Flat universes with one component

Flat, with one component

The Friedmann equation, multiplied by $a^2$:

$$\dot a^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon\, a^2 - \frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}$$
$$\dot a^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\,\varepsilon\, a^2 - \cancel{\frac{\kappa c^2}{R_0^2}}$$
$$\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\,=\,\varepsilon_0 a^{-3(1+w)}]{\;\kappa\,=\,0\;}\quad \dot a^2 = \frac{8\pi G\varepsilon_0}{3c^2}\, a^{-(1+3w)}$$

Try a power law, $a \propto t^q$. The left side goes as $t^{2(q-1)}$, the right side as $t^{-q(1+3w)}$, so

$$2q - 2 = -q(1 + 3w) \quad\Rightarrow\quad q = \frac{2}{3(1+w)}$$

With $a(t_0) = 1$:

$$\textcolor{#ea580c}{a(t) = \left(\frac{t}{t_0}\right)^{2/(3+3w)}}$$
ȧ ∝ t^(q−1), so ȧ² ∝ t^(2q−2); a^(−(1+3w)) ∝ t^(−q(1+3w)). The constant fixes t0 in terms of ε0 (Ryden eq. 5.49).

Two special cases

$$a(t) = \left(\frac{t}{t_0}\right)^{2/(3+3w)}$$
  • $w = -1$ (cosmological constant): the power is infinite. The solution takes a different form: in a few slides.
  • $w < -1$ ("phantom dark energy"): the power is negative. The expanding solution reaches $a = \infty$ after a finite time: the universe ends in a Big Rip.
For w < −1 the expanding solution is a ∝ (t_rip − t)^(2/(3+3w)), with a negative power: a → ∞ as t → t_rip. Everything, down to atoms, is torn apart. Whether w is exactly −1 is an open question (DESI, Lecture 4).

Matter only: the Einstein–de Sitter universe

For matter, $w = 0$:

$$a(t) = \left(\frac{t}{t_0}\right)^{2/3}$$

Q: What is the relation between the Hubble constant $H_0$ and the age of the universe $t_0$?

$$H_0 = \frac{\dot a(t_0)}{a(t_0)} = \frac{2}{3t_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad t_0 = \frac{2}{3}\,H_0^{-1}$$

The universe is younger than the Hubble time.

Problem set 2 used this model. Einstein and de Sitter (1932). The standard model until the 1990s: flat, and only matter. With H0 = 68 km/s/Mpc it gives t0 = 9.6 Gyr: younger than the oldest stars (about 13 Gyr). An "age problem".

Adding matter only

Same slope as the empty universe today (same H0), but bending down: decelerating, so the Big Bang is closer, 2/3 of a Hubble time ago. Today's distance levels off at large z, and the spacetime diagram shows the horizon (green): this universe has a horizon, the edge of the observable universe. Ask: what is the horizon distance? (2c/H0, from the integral of Lecture 4.)

Radiation only

For radiation, $w = 1/3$:

$$a(t) = \left(\frac{t}{t_0}\right)^{1/2}, \qquad t_0 = \frac{1}{2}\,H_0^{-1}$$

In general, for $w > -\tfrac13$ the age is less than the Hubble time, $t_0 < H_0^{-1}$.

Why? These universes decelerate ($\ddot a < 0$, Lecture 4). The expansion was faster in the past, so it took less time than $1/H_0$ to get to today's size.

A radiation-only universe is a good description of our universe early on, for a ≪ a_rm. The next slide shows the deceleration argument as a picture, and the "Try it" slide after de Sitter lets the students explore it.

Adding radiation only

Radiation decelerates even more: the Big Bang is only half a Hubble time ago, and the horizon is closer, c/H0. Order of the curves at fixed z: the more the universe decelerates, the smaller the distances.

Λ only: the de Sitter universe

Now $\varepsilon_\Lambda$ is constant, so the Friedmann equation gives a constant Hubble parameter:

$$H^2 = \left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G\,\varepsilon_\Lambda}{3c^2} = H_0^2$$

Rearrange, $\dfrac{da}{a} = H_0\, dt$, and integrate:

$$a(t) = e^{H_0(t - t_0)}$$

$a \to 0$ only as $t \to -\infty$: no Big Bang, and an infinite age. The universe doubles in size every $\ln 2/H_0 \approx 10$ Gyr.

Willem de Sitter, 1917, the same year as Einstein's static universe. Our universe is heading towards this: Λ will dominate more and more. Inflation (Lecture 13) is a de Sitter phase in the very early universe.

Adding Λ only

Λ bends the other way: accelerating, and no Big Bang. Distances today are the largest of the four: d = z c/H0 exactly, and no horizon. At emission, the distance approaches c/H0 for large z: light from beyond that never reaches us (an event horizon). Next: any w with a slider.
